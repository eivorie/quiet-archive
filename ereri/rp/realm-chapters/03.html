Chapter III

Levi

The royal chamber was warm despite the late hour. Several candles still burned low along the stone walls, their light reflecting faintly against polished metal and silk drapery. Reiner stood near the window overlooking the lake, hands clasped behind his back, watching the last remnants of lantern debris drift toward the shore.

When Levi entered, he did not turn immediately.

“She’s more tired than she admits,” Reiner said at last.

Levi closed the door behind him and waited.

“Fatigue is expected,” he replied evenly. “Her condition is stable.”

Reiner turned then, the weight of private concern visible in the set of his jaw.

“I don’t want stable,” he said. “I want certainty.”

There was no anger in his voice. Only fear carefully restrained.

“I want additional provisions brought to the castle. More medicinal herbs. Higher-grade grains. Strengthening tonics. Anything that reduces risk.”

Levi considered him before answering.

“The current stores are sufficient.”

“For now,” Reiner replied. “But I will not gamble with her health.”

Levi stepped closer to the table between them.

“Increasing requisitions now will be noticed,” he said calmly. “The outer villages are already adjusting to reduced yields from the storm. Further withdrawals will widen that strain.”

Reiner’s gaze sharpened. “Are you suggesting the Queen’s pregnancy should be secondary to village discomfort?”

“I am suggesting,” Levi answered without raising his voice, “that the realm cannot afford to associate her pregnancy with scarcity.”

Silence settled between them.

“If supplies begin to thin visibly after this announcement, resentment will not be directed at the weather.”

Reiner exhaled slowly.

“What would you propose instead?”

Levi did not hesitate.

“Invite representatives from each village. One or two at a time. Let them train here under supervision.”

“In medicine?”

“In preservation. Substitution methods. Efficient preparation using limited ingredients. There are restorative techniques that require no rare crops at all.”

He held the king’s gaze steadily.

“If they return equipped to produce more with less, pressure eases. The castle is seen sharing knowledge rather than extracting resources.”

Reiner remained quiet for several seconds.

“And Historia?” he asked at last.

“She will not lack,” Levi replied. “But neither should the rest of the realm.”

Reiner nodded once.

“Draft the structure,” he said. “We will review it formally.”

Levi inclined his head and withdrew.

Hanji was waiting just beyond the chamber doors, leaning casually against the wall.

“Well?” she asked.

“He wants increased provisions brought in,” Levi said as they began walking down the corridor.

Hanji’s expression shifted, though she masked it quickly.

“Of course he does.”

Levi’s steps remained measured.

“At the festival,” he said, “many of the stalls were stretched thin. Some were decorative more than stocked.”

“You noticed that,” she replied quietly.

“They are managing with less,” Levi continued. “If we take more now, it will not go unnoticed.”

Hanji studied him for a moment.

“So you suggested training.”

“Yes.”

She gave a faint smile.

“You don’t usually volunteer to educate half the countryside.”

“It isn’t education,” Levi replied. “It’s maintenance. And necessity.”

Hanji did not miss the weight beneath that word.

“You’re thinking ahead.”

“I prefer to.”

She walked beside him in silence for a few steps.

“And if one of those villages sends someone… interesting?” she added lightly.

Levi did not respond, which, in itself, was answer enough.

Eren

A week had passed since the lantern festival. Life in the village remained the same—hard work, but the market stalls were becoming increasingly scarce. Some handwritten notices limited the amount each person could buy, especially when it came to medicinal plants.

Eren crossed his arms as he sat at the wooden table while his mother stirred a pot of pumpkin soup, its scent drifting through the village.

“So you’re telling me these rumors are real?”

Eren showed little interest, letting out a deep sigh.

“Not just rumors, my son. The village believes that, if this truly happens, the most appropriate thing would be for you to go represent us. You’re young, intelligent, and you know a lot about medicinal plants. We’re the only stall in the village in this field, Eren. If you refuse, it will be very poorly seen by everyone. People are placing their expectations on you to help us a little.”

She spoke gently and approached Eren, sitting across from him as she watched him.

“I can’t accept this, Mom. You know very well I have no respect for royalty. This only proves they see us as slaves all the time and—”

He was interrupted.

“This is not the moment for those thoughts when you have your family, your friends, and an entire village going through scarcity, Eren. I don’t remember the last time I saw you being this selfish.”

Carla stood up, a little irritated.

“Think about it and give an answer after you reflect. Not everything in life is about being ready immediately—I believe I taught you that.”

Eren stood up.

“I’ll be late tonight. Connie isn’t feeling well, so I promised I’d serve drinks at the tavern. Don’t worry about me.”

Eren left.

Levi

The responses arrived in steady intervals over the following days.

Each village sent a sealed parchment bearing a name, a brief description of skill, and the mark of their local authority. Levi reviewed them in the infirmary, seated at the long central table where dried herbs were usually sorted.

He read each carefully, not for prestige, but for pattern.

Some villages sent elderly healers who had worked the land for decades. Others nominated younger apprentices with practical knowledge of storage and preparation. A few names carried small notes of endorsement—“trustworthy,” “disciplined,” “reliable.”

Levi did not rely on adjectives.

He stacked the parchments in order of arrival and memorized them one by one.

Mikasa stood nearby, hands folded behind her back.

“You went to the southern estates again,” she said quietly.

“Yes.”

She waited.

“The outer fields are producing less than reported,” he continued. “Not enough to alarm the crown, but enough to strain distribution.”

“Blight?”

“No.”

He glanced at her briefly.

“Selective thinning.”

Mikasa’s expression did not change.

“Someone harvesting in small quantities.”

“Yes.”

“Intelligent.”

“Yes.”

Levi placed another parchment atop the pile.

“They’re taking little enough to avoid notice. Far from the central storage.”

Mikasa considered this.

“Organized?”

“Likely.”

“And you think it connects?”

“I think,” Levi said evenly, “that scarcity and coordination rarely happen separately.”

He closed the stack of responses and tied them with cord.

“The trainees will be lodged in the lower east wing,” he added. “Access to the infirmary will be supervised. No one enters storage without clearance.”

Mikasa inclined her head.

“I’ll observe during instruction.”

“Watch their hands,” Levi said. “Not their faces.”

She understood.

Faces lied. Hands hesitated.

Outside, the courtyard was being cleared in preparation for the arrivals. The outer gate guards had received their instructions. Entry would be staggered, documented, controlled.

Three days later, the first delegation approached.

From the upper balcony, Levi watched as the castle gates opened.

Not ceremoniously. Not dramatically.

Just enough.

The wooden doors moved slowly inward, revealing the path that led from the outer road to the inner yard.

One by one, the representatives crossed the threshold.

Levi did not look for familiarity.

He looked for irregularity.

The realm had sent its chosen learners.

And the castle, for the first time in years, allowed the villages to walk inside its walls.

Eren

“Boss, allow me to say this, but how can you accept this?” one of the guards said, while they all watched Eren leaning against the wall, observing the six of them in front of him.

“This is no longer about games. It’s about necessity.”

Eren was brief and practical as the torchlight reflected across the tavern’s hidden room.

“This is very strange. The plan was to steal the crops right after the festival, and you postponed it. You simply said it wasn’t the right moment, and now you want us to think that’s normal? I came from another village for this and made all this effort for you, Eren, and you just—”

Eren interrupted Jean.

“I think you’ve forgotten something. Don’t call me by my name in this hideout, even if it’s only us here. The walls always listen.”

Eren’s gaze was serious.

“I shouldn’t have to explain myself, but you need to understand the problem we faced on the night of the festival. The royal soldiers are not here just to fight. They’re sharp and investigative. We can’t act on impulse anymore. Our minds have to be used here, and this will be the best choice.”

Jean laughed ironically.

“Alright, Eren. Do whatever you think is best. But we don’t have all the time in the world, okay?”

He walked away, while the others remained silent.

“You may leave. We’ll talk more another time,” Eren said, and the men nodded.

The next day.

Eren arrived at the palace gates.

The journey on horseback had been difficult. Some obstacles along the narrow, shorter path had caused him to arrive late.

“Hey, you there, young man! Show your identification letter,” the soldier at the gate stopped him.

Eren said nothing. He simply showed the letter and watched as the soldier slowly stepped aside to allow him in.

“Next time, remember that for matters of the castle you must be punctual—even if this is merely assistance for peasants. The garden is straight ahead.”

Eren chose not to say much.

He simply walked forward, observing every small detail of that place.

Levi

Five representatives had already been received by midday.

They stood in the lower courtyard speaking quietly among themselves while a guard finalized their lodging assignments. The atmosphere was restrained, formal but not hostile.

Levi remained near the infirmary entrance, reviewing a final set of notes. Mikasa stood a few steps behind him, observing the courtyard without appearing to.

Hoofbeats echoed through the stone archway.

Both of them looked up.

A single rider entered the outer gate.

Dust marked his boots and the hem of his cloak. The horse’s flanks were damp from exertion. The rider dismounted with controlled ease, handed over the reins without ceremony, and presented his letter to the guard.

Levi saw his face before he heard the name.

Recognition was immediate.

Lantern light. A narrow street. Bruised skin beneath pale fingers.

There was no outward reaction.

Hanji descended the steps before Levi moved. She approached the newcomer with an open, easy expression.

“You must be from Ashvale,” she said warmly. “The road can be unforgiving this time of year.”

The young man inclined his head slightly.

Hanji smiled at that.

“Let’s have a look at your letter.”

She broke the seal, scanned the parchment, then glanced toward Levi.

“Eren Jäger. Recommended for knowledge in medicinal preparation and plant preservation.”

The name settled where the memory already had.

Levi stepped forward at last.

Their eyes met.

There was no surprise in either of them.

“Your quarters are in the east wing,” Levi said evenly. “Training begins at dawn. You will report to the infirmary.”

Eren held his gaze for a fraction longer than necessary.

“Understood.”

No pleasantries. No references to the festival. Just acknowledgment.

Mikasa shifted slightly as Eren passed her. Her eyes followed him, not with hostility, but with assessment.

Levi did not look away until Eren reached the archway leading to the east wing.

Only then did he turn back toward the courtyard.

Eren

Gray Eyes

Eren walked, following the direction to his quarters. He looked slightly lost. As much as his imagination had tried to picture how large and luxurious the castle might be, he had never imagined such immensity. Although entry to the main castle was not permitted, even the guest quarters conveyed more comfort than his entire village.

Eren observed every detail along the way. He memorized each one carefully.

A few hours passed—enough time for him to settle in before the supposed infirmary training.

Eren left a little earlier than necessary, only to be stopped by another soldier.

“Where are you going?” he asked.

“My training at the infirmary starts in a few minutes.” Eren smiled politely.

“Then why are you leaving your quarters now?” the soldier asked suspiciously.

“Because I didn’t come here only to learn, but also to share my knowledge in order to help the royal household as well. You can follow me there if you’d like. I just want to take my notes. I believe we’re not being kept under house arrest here, are we? Or did Lady Reiss ask you to lock us in the wing that was made precisely for the villagers?” Eren adjusted his tone with a faint smile.

“Go straight ahead and take the next right. You’ll find it in the next corridor. If you stray from the path, you’ll run into a soldier ready to cut your throat, so you should know better,” he replied in a bored tone.

Eren followed the path and soon found a sign pointing to the infirmary.

He stepped inside the place—immense, organized, filled with different kinds of medicinal plants carefully labeled and set aside for analysis.

Eren slowly approached to observe them more closely.

Levi

Levi entered the infirmary and immediately noted the deviation. One representative had arrived before the others.

Ginseng boy stood near the central worktable, posture relaxed but not idle. His eyes moved deliberately along the shelves, across the hanging bundles of dried plants, over the labeled jars arranged by region and rarity.

He was not touching anything.

He was measuring though.

“You were reported absent at dinner,” Levi said evenly.

There was no apology offered in response.

Levi observed the bruising still faint along the side of his neck. The swelling had receded but not entirely. The packet he had given him was no longer visible.

Levi stepped further into the room and noted the small details: the way his gaze had paused on the secondary storage door; the brief assessment of the window height; the angle from which he stood to see both the main table and the corridor entrance.

At the festival, he had watched the crowd in much the same way.

Levi did not comment on it.

“I expected someone older from Ashvale,” he said instead.

The parchment bearing the village seal had listed a medicinal stall as reference. Records in the registry included a secondary name tied to it—Carla Jäger.

Levi had assumed she would come.

He glanced once at the jars containing ginseng, neatly sealed and placed on the upper shelf.

“At the festival,” he added calmly, “you claimed you weren’t particularly skilled at persuading others about herbs.”

The memory was precise.

Now the same young man stood inside the most organized infirmary in the realm as the village’s chosen representative.

Footsteps echoed in the corridor outside as the remaining trainees approached.

Levi moved to the head of the table.

“Take your place,” he said.
